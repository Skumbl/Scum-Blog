My life has been very busy and very different this year. I’m thankful for the changes: I’ve gotten to travel more than ever and spend a lot of time with my beautiful girlfriend. But it means I don’t have the same amount of time to dig deep into the kind of “digital freedom” I used to occupy myself with.

That might sound strange. Does that now mean I use spyware all the time and post everything on Facebook? Well, not really, but... kinda?

When people talk about digital freedom and independence, they rarely talk about the time commitment and learning curve that come with it, let alone the upkeep and upfront cost of hosting your own services. There will always be the argument of “just spin it up on an EC2, it costs nothing,” but I feel that defeats the purpose. You’re still renting someone else’s computer, and you still have to SSH in to maintain it.

An example I keep coming back to is the loss of convenience when I switched to GrapheneOS and used it as my main phone for six months. I couldn’t use any of my banking apps, or a lot of the apps I needed to get by day to day. And I still ended up using half the social media apps I had before, just running them with sandboxed Google Play. Instagram, for example. I understand that the sandbox kept it from my location and other data it would normally gather, but at the end of the day, Instagram still read all of my messages, saw all of my posts, and got whatever metadata was attached to my photos when I uploaded them.

And speaking of photos, not having anything automagically backing up and syncing was a real pain in the ass. I had to set up my Nextcloud to sync everything, and it wasn’t always the fastest. Which, of course, meant more time maintaining a server.

This is a lot of complaining for a single post, so what’s the point of this rant?

Well, I wish things were a little easier. I wish it weren’t so damn hard to get away from big tech. I wish I didn’t have to do so much server upkeep just to keep Google from reading all of my documents. I wish I didn’t have to give up the apps all my friends are on all the time.

I don’t think I’ll ever fully give up on privacy, but I’m not in a phase of my life where I can spend four hours setting up something I know will be more secure, but a little worse than the product I’m leaving.

I’m glad I had this phase, and I think it’s one more people should go through, if nothing else to become a little more security conscious and give the Linux ecosystem a try. But I have an iPhone now, and I pick services based on how useful they are to me.

That doesn’t mean I’ll stop trying to be secure when I can. But the added friction, mixed with my much more limited free time, means I can’t deep dive like I once did.
